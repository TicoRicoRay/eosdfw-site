Rocks vs. To-Dos: why your team keeps missing quarterly priorities.

Most missed EOS Rocks aren’t a discipline problem. They’re a definition problem. Here’s the honest difference between a Rock and a to-do, why 3 to 7 is the magic number, and how to set Q4 priorities your leadership team will actually hit.

By Ray Myers · Professional EOS Implementer® · Dallas–Fort Worth

The end-of-quarter reality

It’s the last week of the quarter. The leadership team pulls up the Rocks board. Half the Rocks are red. Everyone has a story. Sales blames the pipeline. Ops blames sales. Finance blames the software rollout. The CEO makes a note to “tighten up Rock-setting next quarter,” the team promises to do better, and the same thing happens 90 days later.

If that scene looks familiar, you don’t have a discipline problem. You have a definition problem. Almost every missed Rock I see in the field wasn’t actually a Rock. It was a to-do dressed in a Rock’s clothing, or an initiative too big to fit in 90 days. Either way, it was never going to get done, and the team knew it in week two.

What a Rock actually is

In EOS®, a Rock is a 90-day priority. It has five non-negotiable properties:

  1. One owner. Rocks are singular. Shared ownership is no ownership.
  2. SMART. Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time-bound. If you can’t tell on day 90 whether it’s done, it isn’t a Rock.
  3. Outcome, not activity. “Launch the new pricing page” is a Rock. “Work on the pricing page” is a to-do that never ends.
  4. 3 to 7 per person, company-wide. More than 7 means nothing is a priority. Fewer than 3 usually means the team isn’t stretching.
  5. Reviewed weekly. On the Level 10™ Meeting scorecard as on-track or off-track, discussed only when off.

That’s it. If any one of those five is missing, it’s not a Rock. And here’s the uncomfortable part: most leadership teams break at least two of them in every quarterly planning session.

Rock vs. To-Do: the actual difference

This is the part most articles skip, so I’ll be blunt. A Rock is an outcome. A to-do is an action. That’s the whole difference, and it’s why definition matters more than discipline.

  • Rock: “New CRM live and adopted by all sales reps by March 31.”
  • To-do (from that Rock): “Send RFP to three CRM vendors by Feb 3.”

The Rock lives on the quarterly board. The to-dos live in the weekly Level 10 Meeting, one at a time, each with a name and a date. A Rock without to-dos underneath it is a wish. A to-do without a Rock above it is busywork.

Why Rocks slip: the four real reasons

In hundreds of DFW leadership team sessions, missed Rocks almost always trace to one of four causes. In order of frequency:

  1. Too many. The team set 4 company Rocks and each of 6 leaders also set 5 individual Rocks. That’s 34 priorities. Nothing is a priority. By week four, the leaders default to their normal jobs and Rocks quietly fall out of scope.
  2. Not actually Rocks, but initiatives. “Roll out the new operating model.” That’s not a 90-day priority. That’s an 18-month initiative. When you try to squeeze an initiative into a quarter, the definition of “done” gets soft, the owner gets vague, and by day 60 the team is arguing about scope instead of executing.
  3. Unclear finish line. The Rock is “improve customer onboarding.” Improve compared to what? By how much? By when? If two people on the team can look at the same Rock in April and disagree about whether it got done, it wasn’t a Rock.
  4. No weekly check-in. Rocks are supposed to be reviewed every week in the Level 10 Meeting as on-track or off-track. If the review happens monthly, or worse, only at the quarterly, off-track Rocks get discovered in week 10 with no time to recover.

Notice that none of those four are “the team isn’t trying hard enough.” Effort is rarely the issue. Structure almost always is.

The Plano leadership team that cut Rocks in half

A 40-person services firm in Plano came into their Q3 quarterly with 28 Rocks on the board. Their Q2 completion rate: 46 percent. The CEO wanted to know how to “get the team to hit more Rocks.”

We did the opposite. We cut the list.

In the room, we ran each Rock through a two-question filter: is this a 90-day outcome? Is there one owner who can honestly commit? After two hours, 28 became 14. Three of the deleted Rocks turned out to be pieces of a single larger Rock and got consolidated. Five were to-dos in disguise and got moved to the Level 10 to-do list. Six were initiatives too big for a quarter and got broken up, with only the first piece kept as a Rock for the current quarter.

Q3 completion rate: 86 percent. Q4 they stayed disciplined at 15 Rocks and hit 93 percent. Same team. Same effort. Better definitions.

Setting Rocks that will actually get done: the checklist

Use this in your next quarterly planning session, before any Rock leaves the room:

  1. Say the outcome out loud. If it takes more than one sentence, it’s probably an initiative, not a Rock.
  2. Point at the owner. One name. Not a team. Not a department. A person who will be embarrassed if it’s red in 90 days.
  3. Write the finish line. A number, a date, or a demonstrable deliverable. “Better” is not a finish line.
  4. Count the Rocks per person. No leader should own more than 3. If they do, cut something or move it to next quarter.
  5. Stack-rank the company Rocks. If everything is priority one, nothing is. Rank them 1 through N and put the number on the board.
  6. Commit to weekly review. Every Rock, every Level 10, on-track or off-track. Off-track goes to an IDS™ session in the same meeting.

What Rocks don’t solve

Honest pushback, because I owe you that.

Rocks don’t fix a broken Vision. If your V/TO™ is vague or the leadership team doesn’t agree on the 3-Year Picture, better Rocks will just help you sprint in circles. Fix the Vision first, then Rock quarterly toward it.

Sometimes missing a Rock is right. A market shifts in week six. A better opportunity shows up. Killing a Rock mid-quarter with a specific reason is a sign of a mature leadership team. Killing one with no reason on day 88 is a sign of drift. Learn the difference.

Rocks don’t replace project management. A Rock is a commitment on the leadership scoreboard. It doesn’t manage the 40 tasks under it. If your Rock has a real project underneath, you still need a project plan, dependencies, and a check-in cadence tighter than weekly. EOS® is an operating system, not a substitute for execution rigor.

Rocks don’t work without a functional Level 10 Meeting. If your weekly meeting is a status update or a firefight, Rocks will die there quietly. Fix the meeting first. I wrote about how the Level 10 works in 90 minutes if that’s the piece that’s broken.

Try this next quarter

Even without a full EOS® implementation, you can run a stripped-down Rock-setting session on your leadership team:

  1. Block a half day, off-site, no laptops open.
  2. Brainstorm every possible priority for the next 90 days. No filter yet.
  3. Run each candidate through the checklist above. Kill anything that fails.
  4. Cap the survivors at 3 to 7 per person and no more than 7 company Rocks.
  5. Write each one on the board with owner, finish line, and rank number.
  6. Put every Rock on next week’s Level 10 scorecard as on-track by default.

You’ll leave with fewer Rocks than you walked in wanting. That’s the point. Q4 is coming. The teams that hit their Q4 Rocks are the ones with fewer, sharper, better-defined priorities, not the ones with the longest lists.
